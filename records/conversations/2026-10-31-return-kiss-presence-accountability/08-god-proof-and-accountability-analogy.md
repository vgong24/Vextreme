I see the pattern you're pointing toward. **Someone can use a demand for proof as a legitimate search for truth—or as a mechanism for making accountability impossible.**

And your analogy exposes an important distinction: *the same words can serve different functions depending on how the person uses them.*

Consider the two statements:

- “Show me evidence that God exists.”
- “Show me evidence that I committed the crime.”

Both invoke an evidentiary standard. But the question is whether the person asking is genuinely willing to examine evidence, and whether they apply the same standard to their own claims.

A person who commits wrongdoing might demand proof because they know evidence has been concealed or destroyed. In that case, the demand can function as an instrument of evasion.

But someone can also sincerely question God's existence, just as someone accused of a crime can legitimately demand evidence of guilt. **The demand itself doesn't establish dishonesty, wrongdoing, or malicious intent.**

What I find valuable in your analogy is the deeper pattern: **evidence requirements can be weaponized when they're selectively imposed, made impossible to satisfy, or used to dismiss what has already been established.**
