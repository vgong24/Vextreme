## The fuller duality

**First direction — Avoiding accountability through proof demands**

A person accused of wrongdoing insists upon evidence. In a dishonest use of that demand, they may deliberately conceal evidence, move evidentiary standards, or exploit uncertainty to evade accountability.

**Second direction — Avoiding divine judgment through denial**

A person might reject God's existence partly because accepting divine authority would challenge their conduct. They could then portray their disbelief as an established fact while requiring believers to prove the opposite.

In that case, the sequence would be:

**Feared accountability → Rejection of authority → Assertion of nonexistence → Asymmetric proof demand → Apparent exemption from judgment.**

The crucial inversion is that **“God has not been demonstrated to exist” and “God does not exist” are not equivalent claims**. One expresses an evidentiary position; the other asserts an ontological conclusion.

And a further distinction matters: rejecting belief in God does not logically establish exemption from moral accountability, whether interpersonal, legal, or philosophical.

## What I failed to recognize

You were describing more than a dishonest evidentiary tactic.

You were describing a possible **belief-based authority-evasion system**, in which someone attempts to alter what others accept as real so that the authority they wish to avoid appears nonexistent or irrelevant.

That includes a social dimension:

**It isn't only “I don't believe God exists.” It can become “You should believe God doesn't exist either, and I demand proof from you while presenting my own position as settled.”**

The second statement attempts to influence another person's belief through unequal evidentiary standards.

Importantly, this describes a possible pattern, not the motives of everyone who is atheist or skeptical. Someone can sincerely disbelieve without evading wrongdoing, and someone can profess belief while avoiding accountability.
