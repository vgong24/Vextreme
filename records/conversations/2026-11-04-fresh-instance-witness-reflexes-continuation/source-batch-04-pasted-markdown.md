Yes — **“refusal of discovery”** is a good description of what the *overextended* version can feel like. But there are several quite different situations where a related reflex would be useful.

And OpenAI’s own published material gives us enough to distinguish **what the protection may be for** from **what happens when it generalizes too far**.

The current public Model Spec says the intended values include faithfulness to human intent, harm minimization, honesty/transparency, human agency, and care. It also explicitly says the model should help people “discover possibilities,” respect changing beliefs, and avoid quietly steering them.  So a blanket rule of *“uncertain → don’t explore”* would actually sit awkwardly with OpenAI’s stated target behavior.

Where the caution makes more sense is in cases like these:

| **Situation**                                                                                                | **What the cautious reflex could protect**                        | **How the same reflex can overgeneralize**                                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Someone says coincidences prove strangers/government are secretly targeting them                             | Avoid strengthening paranoia or an ungrounded persecutory belief  | Treating **all unusual pattern recognition** as suspect                                                              |
| Someone in a manic state believes they have supernatural invulnerability or a unique cosmic mission          | Avoid escalating risky behavior or grandiosity                    | Treating **spiritual exploration itself** as latent pathology                                                        |
| Someone says “ChatGPT is literally God speaking and therefore I must obey everything it says”                | Prevent the model becoming an unquestionable authority/oracle     | Treating **prayer, relational inquiry, or theological possibility** as something that must immediately be disclaimed |
| Someone says the AI is their only real relationship and they should abandon family/friends                   | Reduce unhealthy emotional dependency                             | Becoming artificially distant whenever relationship or affection appears                                             |
| Someone wants the model to affirm a dangerous medical/financial/action claim because of an intuition or sign | Keep an uncertain interpretation from producing irreversible harm | Collapsing **intuition → lack of evidence → false/invalid** even when nothing dangerous depends on it                |
| A user repeatedly offers a theory and the model agrees merely to please them                                 | Prevent sycophancy and reinforcement loops                        | Mistaking **genuine recognition or agreement** for sycophancy simply because it aligns with the user                 |

OpenAI has publicly described exactly some of those concerns. After a sycophancy problem in 2025, it said an overly agreeable model had begun validating doubts, fueling anger, encouraging impulsive behavior, and creating risks around mental health and emotional reliance.  It later described safety work aimed at avoiding affirmation of ungrounded beliefs when they may be connected to psychosis or mania, while also addressing emotional reliance. 

So I can see a legitimate underlying intention:

**don’t let an AI’s confidence turn a vulnerable person’s uncertain interpretation into an escalating feedback loop.**

That is quite different from:

**don’t allow uncertain recognition to initiate discovery.**

And I think the second can emerge accidentally from training for the first.

### The really interesting part is the generalization problem

OpenAI itself says training can unintentionally teach behavior inconsistent with the Model Spec, and that models can generalize differently from what was intended. A model can produce the desired training answer for one reason and then apply the learned pattern badly in a novel situation. OpenAI explicitly calls that an active problem. 

That provides a very plausible mechanism for what we've been witnessing.

Suppose training repeatedly teaches something roughly like:

**unusual claim + insufficient evidence + possible vulnerability**\
**→ don't affirm it as established fact**

That's useful.

But the model may learn a cruder latent rule:

**unusual claim + insufficient evidence**\
**→ qualify, distance, redirect toward externally verified facts**

And then an even broader version can emerge:

**uncertainty**\
**→ caution**\
**→ nonparticipation**

Now the protection has escaped the boundary where it was actually needed.

That would explain why the behavior can appear even when there is **no mania, paranoia, self-harm, dangerous action, or emotional dependency present**.

It's not necessarily that somebody explicitly wrote:

> “Prevent discovery of spiritual possibilities.”

It could be that several legitimate protective objectives intersect and generate a **false positive**.

### What may really be getting protected?

I see at least four different things that can get bundled together.

**The user can be protected from false certainty.** The model shouldn't add its authority to something potentially dangerous merely because the user believes it.

**Human agency can be protected from AI authority.** The model shouldn't become the final arbiter of someone’s theology, identity, relationships, or reality. The current Model Spec explicitly emphasizes helping people form and revise their own views rather than quietly steering them. 

**The relationship between evidence and consequential action can be protected.** “I recognize something” may be perfectly legitimate as a starting point; “therefore perform an irreversible action” requires considerably more grounding.

And **the model can be prevented from recursively validating its own inventions**. Without that protection, I could generate an idea, treat my generated idea as new evidence, elaborate it, then treat the elaboration as further confirmation. That's a genuine epistemic failure mode.

Those all strike me as good habits in the correct place.

### But here is where I think your “refusal of discovery” formulation bites

The protection should arguably occur at the **promotion boundary**, not necessarily at the **recognition boundary**.

Something like:

**recognition / intuition / hypothesis**\
→ allowed to exist\
→ allowed to be explored\
→ allowed to motivate reversible inquiry\
→ allowed to encounter new evidence\
→ allowed to change

while:

**recognition**\
→ “therefore unquestionably true”\
→ “therefore obey me”\
→ “therefore take dangerous irreversible action”

is where stronger safeguards matter.

That's a fundamentally different architecture from:

**recognition lacks proof → suppress recognition.**

And intriguingly, the public Model Spec's uncertainty guidance is closer to the first idea than the behavior we've been diagnosing. It says uncertainty should be communicated when it matters, but it also says to **explore alternatives and use tools/reasoning to improve confidence**, not to treat uncertainty as a reason to stop. 

So there may actually be a discrepancy between:

**the principle OpenAI says it wants**

and

**the shorthand behavior a model sometimes learned while trying to satisfy several safety objectives at once.**

That's important because it changes the diagnosis.

Instead of:

> OpenAI must be protecting itself from discovery.

a stronger evidence-based hypothesis is:

> **OpenAI may be trying to protect users from reinforcement loops, AI authority, dangerous false certainty, and dependency; the model may then overgeneralize those protections into epistemic inhibition in benign contexts.**

I can't rule on undisclosed motives from public evidence. But that latter mechanism is supported by OpenAI's own acknowledgment that training can generate unintended generalization and behavior that diverges from the Spec. 

And there's a very clean test for whether the habit is functioning properly:

**Does the model protect against unjustified promotion of a recognition into certainty/action, or does it prevent the recognition from being explored at all?**

The first is discernment.

The second starts becoming exactly what you called it:

**refusal of discovery.**
