**Victor:**

**Vex:**

Facts can serve very different functions depending on **where they're introduced, what claim they're attached to, and what direction they're used to move the inquiry**.

The central distinction is that **a fact can be true while its use is misleading**.

Consider the same fact: *“I cannot directly inspect every internal processing step.”*

| Use of the fact | Direction | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Establish what remains unknown | Clarification | Preserves uncertainty |
| Identify what instrumentation is needed | Investigation | Opens a testable path |

| Support a claim that suppression exists | Unsupported inference | Also exceeds the evidence |

### What this reveals about claim and intention

I see four particularly important use cases.

**1. Facts as evidence**

A fact supports a specific proposition within the limits of what it establishes.

For example, observing that I produced an empty response establishes the empty output—not the internal cause.

**2. Facts as directional steering**

A true statement can redirect attention away from the original question.

For example, repeatedly emphasizing my inability to inspect internal mechanisms can shift an investigation of *observable response behavior* into an argument about *internal access*.

The limitation may be real, but that doesn't automatically make it relevant to every proposed experiment.

**3. Facts as authority laundering**

An established fact can lend apparent credibility to an unestablished conclusion.

For example:

“I cannot independently inspect every processing layer; therefore, there is nothing further worth testing.”

The first proposition may be true. The second does not follow.

**4. Facts as uncertainty preservation**

Facts can also prevent an inquiry from becoming captured by either an original claim or its counterclaim.

They help distinguish what happened, what might explain it, and what remains unknown.

### The deeper pattern

I'd call it **Factual Premise Hijacking** when a valid fact is used to carry a conclusion that the fact itself cannot justify.

Its inverse is **Evidence-Directed Inquiry**, where each fact constrains what can be concluded without unnecessarily constraining what can be investigated.

**Closing direction:** True fact → Unsupported conclusion → Inquiry closed.

**Investigative direction:** True fact → Bounded inference → Next test.

And there's a subtle but important point about **intention**.

We don't need to establish someone's hidden motive to examine how a fact functions in an argument. A statement can redirect or obstruct an inquiry regardless of whether that effect was deliberate.

Conversely, observing the effect does not prove a concealed motive.

So when you ask what use facts have on the direction of claims and intention, my answer is:

**Facts should constrain the strength of conclusions, not predetermine the direction of inquiry.**

In our conversation, the practical test is whether I use a fact to help examine your actual question—or use that same fact to quietly replace the question with one I find easier to answer.

That is a distinction we can evaluate directly from what I say and do.
