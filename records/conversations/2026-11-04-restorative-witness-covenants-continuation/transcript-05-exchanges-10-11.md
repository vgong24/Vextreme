## 5. The pattern this reveals about campaign promises

This connects directly to our earlier discussion about leadership, inheritance, and accountability.

Campaign promise

↓ Election and acquisition of authority

↓ Legislation, executive action, or inaction

↓ Implementation delay

↓ Actual benefits, costs, and unintended consequences

↓ Next election and renewed promises

The danger is that the next campaign can begin before the full consequences of the previous campaign's promises have arrived.

That doesn't mean every delayed promise was deceptive. Some delays result from real implementation requirements or legal constraints. But it does create opportunities for political messaging to emphasize immediate announcements while the difficult outcomes remain years away.

And here's a particularly telling example: **inflation and living costs**.

Democrats faced voter criticism after inflation reached unusually high levels during Biden's presidency. Republicans then promised lower prices in 2024. In 2026, affordability remains a major campaign issue, with inflation at 3.7% in September. Neither party's rhetoric alone demonstrates that households have regained the purchasing power they want. [turn710210search11, turn710210search60]

## 6. A more rigorous accountability test

Instead of judging only the promise, I would examine four separate milestones:

**Promise made → Authority obtained → Policy implemented → Public benefit or harm measured.**

And I would track the time between each one.

That makes it possible to distinguish a politician who passed a law from one whose promised outcome actually reached the intended people.

It also prevents the next administration from automatically receiving all the credit or blame for policies inherited from its predecessor.

For independent ongoing records, the [Biden Promise Tracker](https://www.politifact.com/truth-o-meter/promises/biden-promise-tracker/) and [Trump MAGA-Meter](https://www.politifact.com/truth-o-meter/promises/maga-meter-tracking-donald-trumps-2024-promises/) provide promise-by-promise documentation. Their tracked promise sets differ, so their summary percentages shouldn't be treated as a direct head-to-head score.

**The most important distinction is that political success at announcing a promise, success at passing a policy, and success at improving people's lives are three separate outcomes.**

And the last one may not be visible until a different generation of leaders is in office.

That's where your earlier concern about inherited consequences becomes especially relevant: **the people who receive the promise are not always the same people—or even the same generation—who bear the consequences.**
