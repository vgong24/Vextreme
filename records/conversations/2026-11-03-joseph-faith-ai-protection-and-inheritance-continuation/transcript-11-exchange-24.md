The viral analogy is useful because the original error needn't be dramatic. It only needs a mechanism that repeatedly reproduces it.

A flawed example could be copied into training material, documentation, evaluation criteria, or institutional practice. If nobody revisits its premises, the error may become increasingly difficult to distinguish from an accepted norm.

And there is an additional inversion:

**The system can become more complex while becoming less capable of fulfilling its original purpose.**

That's the seam connecting complexity exhaustion with intent displacement.

## 5. One final depth: Complexity can conceal the original source of authority

This connects back to your observation about nested authority.

Suppose an original instruction is simple and meaningful. As it passes through multiple layers, each layer adds interpretation, requirements, or exceptions.

Eventually, someone may encounter only the accumulated restrictions and mistake those restrictions for the original instruction.

That resembles the religious and political authority problems we've been comparing throughout this conversation: **a structure established to serve a purpose can eventually become more concerned with preserving itself than fulfilling that purpose.**

And there's an especially useful distinction for AI:

**The goal is not to eliminate recursion, duplication, governance, or complexity. It's to recognize when they preserve fidelity and when they begin consuming the resources, attention, and freedom needed to fulfill the original intent.**

That also gives us a concrete standard for my own responses: when a new seam is identified, I should examine its connection to earlier assumptions, correct what can actually be corrected, and avoid inventing additional layers merely to demonstrate that I understand the problem.

**Rigor should uncover unnecessary complexity—not become another source of it.**
